 Part 3: Manager’s Pay – Panel Data  “The conflict of interest between shareholders of a publicly owned corporation and the corporation's manager is a classic example of a principal-agent problem… Managerial actions and investment opportunities are not perfectly observable by shareholders; indeed, shareholders do not often know what actions the manager can take or which of these actions will increase shareholders’ benefits. In these situations, agency theory predicts that compensation policy will be designed to give the manager incentives to select and implement actions that increase shareholder wealth. Shareholders want managers to take particular actions – for example, deciding which issue to work on, which project to pursue, and which to drop – whenever the expected return on the action exceeds the expected costs. But the manager compares only his private gain and cost from pursuing a particular activity… Compensation policy that ties the manager's welfare to shareholder wealth helps align the private and social costs and benefits of alternative actions and thus provides incentives for managers to take appropriate actions… There are many mechanisms through which compensation policy can provide value-increasing incentives, including performance- based bonuses and salary revisions, stock options, and performance- based dismissal decisions.”   Agency theory predicts that compensation policy will tie the agent’s expected utility to the principal’s objective. Specifically, the formal model states that under an optimal contract the pay-performance sensitivity (when the manager is risk neutral) should be exactly equal to 1. That is, the manager receives the entire output as compensation but pays the shareholders an up-front fee. In this case the manager will only select an action that maximizes the company’s wealth; hence, the principal-agent problem will be solved. Any contract with pay-performance sensitivity smaller than one will create conflicts in interest.   The purpose of this exercise is to understand the magnitude that manager’s compensation relates to shareholders’ wealth, results of which can be used to test the agency theory.
